# Lesson 05 — What moves freight rates

**Goal:** learn the supply/demand framework and the seasonal patterns.

## The master equation

> **Freight rate ≈ demand for ships − supply of ships**

Demand is measured in **tonne-miles** (tonnes × distance): a Brazilian iron-ore cargo counts roughly double an Australian one because the voyage is twice as long. Supply is the fleet's ability to carry those tonne-miles. When demand exceeds available capacity, rates spike; when ships outnumber cargoes, rates collapse.

## Demand side (why people need ships)

1. **China** — ~70% of seaborne iron ore; steel and property demand drive everything Capesize.
2. **Commodity seasons** — grain harvests (S. America Q1, N. America Q3–Q4), coal restocking, fertilizer windows.
3. **Infrastructure / stimulus** — government building programs boost steel and thus iron ore.
4. **Substitution** — e.g. if Brazil has a bad crop, the US fills the gap → different (often longer) routes.

## Supply side (how many ships are available)

1. **Fleet size** — how many ships exist (grows slowly, ~2–3%/yr).
2. **Orderbook & scrapping** — newbuilds arrive 2–3 years after ordering; old ships are sent to scrap when rates can't cover fuel and crew. Scrapping is freight's "supply safety valve".
3. **Speed ("slow steaming")** — at low rates owners sail slower to burn less fuel → effectively removes capacity; at high rates they speed up.
4. **Congestion & delays** — port queues, canal issues (Suez, Panama), weather — all tie up ships and tighten supply.
5. **Ballast legs** — when a ship must reposition empty (e.g. Brazil → Australia), that non-earning time reduces effective supply.

## Seasonality — the classic calendar

| Period | Typical driver | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Brazil rains, Australia cyclones, Chinese New Year lull | Weakest rates |
| Q2–Q3 | Grain seasons, gradual steel recovery | Firmer |
| Q4 | Iron-ore restocking + N. American grain + weather risks | The famous **"Q4 rally"** |

These are tendencies, not rules — and everyone knows them, which is why forward curves (Lesson 06) already price them in.

## BDI as an economic indicator

The BDI famously peaked months *before* the 2008 crash — it's a great **barometer** of global trade. But it's a spot price of ship capacity, not a stock: there's no company behind it, it's brutally cyclical, and it can stay at extremes for a long time. Use it as information, not as a market-timing oracle.

## Key takeaways

- Rates = tonne-mile demand vs. fleet supply.
- Demand: China/iron ore, seasons, stimulus. Supply: fleet, orderbook, scrapping, speed, congestion.
- Q1 is typically weak; Q4 often rallies.
- The BDI is an informative trade barometer — not a reliable timing tool on its own.
